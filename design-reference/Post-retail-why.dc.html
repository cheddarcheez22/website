Why foot traffic still needs tracking

By ContentMatter · 3 min read · Published September 25, 2026 · Updated September 30, 2026

Online shopping keeps growing, but it is still a minority of retail sales. For a local store, the customer who walks in is the business, and you can measure more of that path than you might think.

Stores still carry most of retail

The Census Bureau reported that ecommerce made up 17.1% of total retail sales in the second quarter of 2026 on a seasonally adjusted basis. That means more than 80% of retail sales happened through other channels, including physical stores. Ecommerce is growing faster than total retail (12.2% against 6.7% year over year), so it is worth watching, but your store remains the center of the business.

Local searches lead to visits

A Google and Purchased study from 2016, called Digital Diary, found that 76% of people who search on their smartphones for something nearby visit a business within a day, and 28% of those searches result in a purchase. The study is older and behavior has shifted since, but the direction is clear: nearby searches are short, and they lead to action quickly.

The path to your door is visible

BrightLocal's 2025 Consumer Search Behavior survey found that 49% of consumers often or always plan a route to a business after searching. Directions requests, calls, and website clicks on your Google Business Profile record those moments, and Google Ads can count them as local actions when you run ads.

What to do with it

Look at the weekly count of directions requests, calls, and website clicks. Compare them to your door count or sales for the same week. They will not match exactly, but the pattern shows whether your online presence is sending people in.

Pair it with the register

Ask customers how they found you at checkout, or use a simple offer code. The answer is rough, but it ties online activity to real sales.

An illustrative example

A gift shop starts running local ads and sees more people through the door, but cannot tell which ads helped. By watching directions requests and calls on its Google profile and asking customers at the register how they found the shop, the owner sees that the week after a new photo set and a weekend event post, directions climbed and Saturday sales rose. The pattern is rough, but it is usable.

Common mistakes to avoid

Assuming only online sales can be measured, ignoring the register as a data source, judging ads by clicks alone, and forgetting that store visits often follow a phone search within hours. Tracking does not need to be perfect to be useful.

A simple plan for the next 30 days

Week one, record your directions, calls, and website clicks from Google for the last four weeks. Week two, add a simple question at checkout about how customers found you. Week three, compare the weekly activity numbers with weekly sales and look for patterns. Week four, choose one change, such as new photos or a post about an event, and watch the numbers for a full week afterward. Over time, the link between online activity and foot traffic will become clearer, and your marketing choices will rest on evidence.

Try this first

Record your Google Business Profile directions, calls, and website clicks for the last four weeks. Put your weekly sales beside them and look for a pattern.
