Why repeat guests matter more than new ones

By ContentMatter · 3 min read · Published September 10, 2026 · Updated September 30, 2026

Research across industries shows that small gains in retention lead to large gains in profit. For restaurants working with tight margins and softer traffic, the guests you already have are your most reliable growth.

The retention math

Harvard Business Review reported research by Bain & Company's Frederick Reichheld showing that increasing customer retention rates by 5% increases profits by 25% to 95%. That finding comes from across industries, not restaurants alone, but the logic holds for any business where margins are thin: a returning customer costs less to win and is more predictable than a new one.

Why it matters now

The National Restaurant Association's 2026 State of the Restaurant Industry report found that 42% of operators were not profitable last year, and 60% reported softer customer traffic. When traffic is soft, spending to attract strangers gets harder to justify. Giving existing guests a reason to return is often the faster path.

Build a way to reach guests

You cannot invite back someone you cannot contact. Collect an email address or mobile number at the points where guests already engage: online orders, reservations, wait list sign ups, and receipts. Be clear about what they will receive and how often.

Keep the follow up simple

A welcome message after a first visit, a reminder after a quiet stretch, and an occasional note about a new menu or seasonal dish is enough to start. Keep each message short and specific. Track how many people come back after each one, not just how many open it.

Measure return visits

Pick one number to watch: the share of guests who visit again within 60 days. Review it monthly. If it rises, your hospitality and follow up are working. If it is flat, change one thing at a time and watch again.

An illustrative example

A neighborhood restaurant serves 200 guests in a busy week. Most are new faces from a promotion. A month later, few have returned, because the restaurant had no way to reach them. Compare that to a restaurant that captures contact details on online orders and sends one friendly message two weeks later. Even a modest return rate from that list can outperform a new ad campaign at lower cost.

Common mistakes to avoid

Spending only on new guest promotions, discounting so heavily that regulars wait for deals, collecting contacts without ever using them, and sending messages that read like advertising instead of hospitality. A good return message sounds like the host remembering your name.

A simple plan for the next 30 days

Week one, count how many guests you can contact and add a sign up option to your online ordering, reservations, and receipts. Week two, write a short welcome message and a short "we miss you" message. Week three, send the welcome message to new contacts and the reminder to guests who have not been in for a while. Week four, count how many returned and note what they ordered. Repeat the cycle each month, and add a small thank you for your most loyal guests once you have a baseline.

Try this first

Count how many guests from last month you could contact today by email or text. If the number is small, make collecting contact details your first project.
